# Lesson 12.6 — Mentoring, Pairing, Sponsorship

> **Concept first:** mentoring teaches; pairing builds judgment;
> sponsorship opens rooms. You'll do all three.
> **Time:** 15 minutes.

## Mentoring

**Goal:** help someone get better at *their* job.

The mechanics:

- 30 minutes, weekly or biweekly.
- The mentee owns the agenda. You bring topics if they don't.
- Listen more than you talk (~2:1).
- End with one explicit action item per side.

A first-mentoring meeting goes like:

> Mentor: "What's the thing you most want to be better at six months
> from now?"
> Mentee: "I want to feel confident designing systems."
> Mentor: "OK. Where do you feel least confident today?"
> ...

Don't fix the problem in the first meeting. Ask the next-level question.

## Pairing

**Goal:** transfer judgment by doing work together.

Two modes:

- **Driver/navigator.** One types, one reviews live. Switch every 25
  minutes.
- **Strong-style.** "For an idea to go from your head to the computer,
  it must go through someone else's hands." The expert *cannot type*;
  the learner *cannot decide what to type*.

Strong-style is uncomfortable; it teaches more than any review. Use it
when you want to transfer judgment, not just code.

## Sponsorship

**Goal:** put your reputation behind someone in a room they're not in.

Mentoring is about them. Sponsorship is about them in *contexts they
don't yet have access to*. Examples:

- Recommending them to lead a project that stretches them.
- Suggesting their name when someone asks "who could review this?"
- Speaking up in a calibration meeting: "I think they should be
  promoted; here's the evidence."

Sponsorship costs more than mentoring — your reputation is on the line.
Pay it deliberately to people whose work you've reviewed and whose
judgment you trust.

## The growth ladder

Companies usually publish one. Read yours. The mentor's job:

- Help the mentee identify *which level they're at* honestly.
- Help them identify *which behaviors of the next level* they don't
  yet exhibit.
- Help them build evidence.

Avoid: "you'll get promoted in 6 months." Promotions aren't promised by
mentors; they're earned and decided by committees.

## Pitfalls

- **Mentoring as therapy.** It's not. Refer them to actual support if
  they need it.
- **Pairing as constant supervision.** Suffocating. Pair on *specific
  hard problems*; otherwise let them work.
- **Sponsorship of unprepared people.** It backfires on both of you.
  Sponsor when the evidence justifies it.
- **Mentoring at the wrong altitude.** A senior engineer mentoring a
  junior on architecture wastes both their time. Match level to need.

## What good mentees do

(Tell them this in the first meeting.)

- Send the agenda 24 hours before.
- Bring specific blocks, not vague unease.
- Try the advice before deciding it doesn't apply.
- Loop back next session: "I tried X. Here's what happened."
- End with one explicit ask: "Could you connect me with someone who's
  done Y?"

## Why this matters

- **Scaling your hands is mentoring.**
- **Scaling your judgment is pairing.**
- **Scaling your reputation is sponsorship.**

A Principal who doesn't do all three is just a very senior senior.

## Green-bar checkpoint

- You can articulate the difference between mentoring and sponsorship.
- You can pick driver/navigator vs strong-style for a given pairing.
- You can name three things a good mentee does.
